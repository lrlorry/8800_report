\subsection{A useful representation exposes a useful unit of reuse}

The Cartesian codebook permits an exact coordinate decomposition of each
primary table into two 16-entry tables. The newer sweep favours that choice
in 22/24 cells, while four- and eight-way splits store the same 16 entries
per subspace and perform different numbers of lookups
(\cref{fig:7-lut}). The representation therefore makes a compact scorer
possible, but table size alone fails to determine its best implementation.
The relevant reusable unit is the query-specific coordinate group: its
distances are computed once and reused across candidates.

Shared-memory feasibility explains an important resource transition. Full
tables exceed the available space after scan scratch in the evaluated
configurations; two-group tables fit. Beyond that transition, more lookups
and accumulation steps compete with further state reduction. Packed words
likewise change instruction and addressing work while preserving logical
code bytes. These observations support joint consideration of storage,
instructions and reuse. They do not distinguish cache, bank, register and
issue effects without counters. The two exceptional LUT cells and the
Mailman case below are useful counterexamples to a universal smallest-table
rule. Keeping two groups as the default follows the measured trade-off;
it does not identify a device-independent optimum.

The opportunity is narrower than arbitrary PQ: the product here exists
\emph{inside} each primary subcodebook. A learned PQ subspace generally
contains coupled centroids, so splitting the label bits does not split its
distance. Conversely, changing a codebook to improve reconstruction can
remove this execution advantage. A representation should therefore be
assessed by the attainable recall--cost frontier of its scorer and residual
hierarchy, alongside reconstruction error. This connects the mathematical
compatibility check in Chapter 5 to the end-to-end question in Chapter 7.

\subsection{Useful parallelism is bounded by duplicated state and selection}

Integrated scheduling improves geometric-mean throughput by $2.727\times$
at batch 8 and only $1.015\times$ at batch 1,000 against the lane-refinement
reference (\cref{fig:7-batch}). Splitting one query across blocks provides
work when a batch exposes too little query-level parallelism. Each extra
group also brings local buffers, repeated query/table staging and merging.
When the original grid already supplies enough blocks, these costs can
consume the benefit. This interpretation explains the batch trend without
treating maximum occupancy as the objective.

Load skew supplies a separate reason for grouping. At E1's full batch,
only Vogue takes the grouped route, with candidate-count CV 0.338--0.622.
Its matched-recall ratio to RaBitQ at 0.97 changes from 0.933 for one block
to 1.059 for the scheduled path (\cref{tab:7-e1-paths}). The paired runs
therefore connect execution design to a competitive operating region.
They measure the combined scheduling, retention, bound and merge path;
isolating the shared bound requires its own ablation. The schedule also
loses at some shallow-probe Vogue settings, and its regret reaches 1.217
on BGE at batch 256. CV captures imbalance, but omits absolute work and
the cost of staging and merging.

A practical consequence is to retain a query-centric path and choose
grouping from both available parallelism and expected work per query.
Candidate count, survivor capacity and local-buffer cost are plausible
inputs for a richer decision. A fitted selector would need held-out
workloads and its own calibration-cost accounting; the present observations
do not validate one. The existing heuristic is valuable precisely because
its gains and failure cells can be checked separately.

\subsection{Execution speed changes the value of avoiding refinement}

Budget reduction removes residual reads, arithmetic and final selection,
while routing and broad primary-score production remain. Its potential
saving depends on the fraction of latency occupied by those removable
operations. Faster lane-cooperative refinement can consequently reduce the
reward for lowering the survivor budget. Faster primary scanning can have
the opposite effect on refinement's relative share. Neither transformation
changes the quality consequence of discarding a particular candidate.

This is a cost-model implication, rather than a causal explanation for
every historical/newer budget difference. The ADC manuscript's older
calibration times and speedups belong to different execution records.
Comparing those summaries with the newer v69 measurements changes several
conditions simultaneously. A causal test would replay identical budget
decisions and candidate sets while changing only the refinement kernel.
Likewise, selected-arm QPS is measured independently of calibration; net
service savings require enough reuse; direct four-cell traces now measure
that boundary
(\cref{sec:6-5}).

The newer FULL results establish a useful observed quality-first working
point: all twelve cells meet the tested $10^{-3}$ relative-loss target.
Smaller samples reduce calibration cost but sometimes accept harmful
budgets. The independent-slot resolution calculation motivates a sample
scale near 500, while its stronger independence assumption and the
conditional ordering assumptions remain explicit; supplementary FULL
enumeration agrees in all twelve cells. The engineering
decision is to spend calibration effort according to a workload's quality
requirement and expected reuse. Optimising the selector alone, or reporting
only a favourable selected-arm mean, leaves that decision incomplete.
