\subsection{Learning a representation versus exploiting its structure}

Optimised product quantisation learns the space decomposition and codebooks
to reduce quantisation distortion \cite{opq}. Its rotation is optimised for
the data, whereas JHQ's random square rotation supports a cheap analytical
primary model. The trade-off involves fitting cost, reconstruction fidelity
and scorer structure. Improving a general learned codebook does not
automatically preserve the internal Cartesian product needed by an exact
16+16 decomposition.

ScaNN's anisotropic quantisation work instead weights error according to
its relevance to maximum-inner-product retrieval, showing why reconstruction
MSE is not the only useful objective \cite{scann}. Its objective and
implementation setting differ from the squared-L2 hierarchical pipeline
here. The useful comparison is the choice of loss: a rank-sensitive
representation should be tested against actual retained-neighbour quality
and execution cost. The stopped rank-oriented experiment in Chapter 8
provides an example of this distinction within JHQ.

\subsection{Small lookup tables have substantial prior art}

PQ Fast Scan replaces reliance on cache-resident tables with tables suited
to CPU SIMD registers \cite{fastscan}. Quicker ADC generalises register
lookup techniques and introduces irregular quantisers and split tables
\cite{quickeradc}. These works establish that table shape and the execution
instruction set must be designed together. This thesis's claim is the exact
decomposition available from its inherited Cartesian coordinate structure,
and its measured implementation on a GPU. CPU SIMD results are conceptual
precedents, not throughput measurements on the tested GPU. FP32 table
association and cutoff ties still require separate numerical checking.

RaBitQ also uses randomised quantisation and compact sign information,
with a distance estimator and theoretical error analysis
\cite{rabitq}. Multi-bit extensions trade storage for estimator accuracy
\cite{rabitq_multi}. Their guarantees attach to their specified constructions
and estimator assumptions. They cannot be transferred to JHQ merely because
both use rotations and binary components. In JHQ, primary signs select
survivors and an explicitly stored residual supplies the next precision
level; the resulting quality depends on both truncation and reconstruction.

GPU IVF-RaBitQ brings this family into a complete GPU build/search design,
including GPU-oriented distance computation and fused search
\cite{ivfrabitq}. It is therefore a particularly relevant contemporary
baseline, rather than background evidence of a missing GPU quantiser.
Its fused execution makes clear that compact codes alone are insufficient:
the access and selection pipeline must also be competitive. E1 includes
multiple bit budgets, and its high-recall results limit any broad claim
of JHQ dominance.

\subsection{Refinement effort and adaptive stopping}

Candidate expansion and reranking already appear in Faiss's
\texttt{k\_factor} and cuVS refinement controls
\cite{faiss_refine,cuvs_refinement}. Faiss autotuning can use GT-labelled
queries to search recall--time trade-offs \cite{faiss_autotune}. Learned
adaptive termination predicts sufficient search effort
\cite{li_termination}; DARTH frames early termination around declarative
recall targets \cite{darth}. These are relevant alternatives to spending a
uniform effort everywhere. Chapter 6's particular policy selects one
workload-level survivor multiplier using unlabelled agreement with a larger
budget. It avoids a trained predictor, while inheriting the reference's
quality limits. This distinction is the policy's scope, rather than a claim
that adaptive search or refinement budgets are new ideas.
